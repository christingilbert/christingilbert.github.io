Case study — product & interaction design

Nookfare.

A field journal, not a marketplace.

Nookfare helps travellers understand how a stay might feel before they book it — trading star ratings for plain evidence: what's known, who reported it, and what still needs checking. This is the design system, the product logic, and the working prototype behind it.

01 The problem

Booking sites are built to move you fast, not to help you feel sure.

Countdown timers. "Only 1 left at this price." Star ratings that average away exactly the details that matter most — how loud the street is, how bright the room gets at 6 a.m., whether check-in means a text message or a stranger meeting you at the door. For travellers who get overwhelmed easily — sensory sensitivity, decision fatigue, AuDHD — that combination of urgency and vagueness is exhausting before the trip even starts.

Nookfare asks a narrower, more honest question: not "is this a good deal," but "how might this stay feel, and what do I still need to check before I'm comfortable booking it." Every screen exists to answer one part of that question, and nothing else.

02 Principles

Five rules the product isn't allowed to break.

  1. 01

    Evidence before verdict

    A match label never appears alone. "Strong match" is always followed by why — "Matches 5 of the preferences you shared" — never a bare percentage or a star rating standing in for a real reason.

  2. 02

    Uncertainty is visible, not hidden

    "Not yet verified" sits in the same rank as confirmed facts, just quieter — a dashed outline instead of a hard border. Unknown information is still information; hiding it would be its own kind of lie.

  3. 03

    No dark patterns, ever

    No countdown timers, no fake scarcity, no manufactured urgency. If a decision needs to feel calmer, the fix is clearer information — never a nudge.

  4. 04

    One colour, one meaning

    Deep terracotta marks exactly one thing: you can act here. It never grades a stay, so a traveller never has to decode what a colour is trying to tell them about quality.

  5. 05

    Booking stays external — on screen, not in fine print

    Nookfare never processes payment or confirms availability. That boundary is stated plainly on the booking screen itself, not buried in a terms page nobody reads.

Wordmark, page titles, property names — Espresso ink, never body copy.

Interface — Nunito Sans

Matches 5 of the preferences you shared. Traveller reported.

Everything functional: body, values, buttons, navigation, source labels.

The rule that holds it together: terracotta appears only on things a traveller can act on — buttons, links, selection, focus. It's never used to say a stay is good or bad. That job belongs to the qualitative match bands alone: Strong, Possible, Lower — never a percentage, never a star.

04 The flow

Four screens, one continuous decision.

Discovery

The list shows it continues

Every card leads with the match band and the exact reason behind it — never a bare label. Cards are sized so one sits complete and the next peeks in above the fold: no "load more" button, no doubt that there's more to see.

  • Match band + exact preference count, always paired
  • Source label on its own line — never squeezed beside the fact
  • The compare tray never covers a card's own actions

Evaluation

Uncertainty gets one honest sentence

"2 details still need checking" is never a separate, alarming section — it's one calm, tappable disclosure with a next step attached. Below it, the Sensory Snapshot answers a single question per row: noise, light, scent, crowds, check-in — each with its source, never asserted without one.

  • One uncertainty disclosure, not a scattered warning
  • Compact value-and-source rows — nothing repeats, nothing shrinks
  • The external-booking boundary stated on the screen itself

Decision

Differences first, agreement collapsed

Comparing three stays shouldn't mean reading fifteen identical rows. Categories where every stay agrees fold into one quiet disclosure; everything that actually differs stays open, grouped by what kind of difference it is — sensory environment, then arrival and communication.

  • "2 categories differ" — a number, not a wall of text
  • Matching categories named, not just counted, before they collapse
  • No stay is ever declared the winner

Commitment

One state at a time, never two at once

Before saving, there's exactly one action: Save to Vault. After saving, that button is gone — replaced by a status, a quieter way back in, and a reversible way out. The traveller is never shown two conflicting instructions in the same breath.

  • Reversible actions never wear the interaction colour
  • Removal only asks for confirmation when there's something real to lose
  • Booking status (yes / no / undecided) never blocks saving

05 A quieter fix

The most useful bug I found wasn't visual.

Partway through this project, the Selected Stay screen was showing Save to Vault and View in Vault at the same time — always, even on a stay that had never been saved. The cause: the app already tracks "the stay currently being reviewed" for the booking flow, and that record existed by the time this screen loaded. The old code mistook a record exists for the traveller chose to save this. Those are different facts, and only one of them should ever put a button on screen.

Field note

Before

Both actions always visible, regardless of whether the traveller had saved anything — an unsaved stay looked identical to a saved one.

After

A single explicit savedToVault flag, set only by the traveller's own click. Exactly one state renders at a time, and a second pass caught a leftover confirmation banner duplicating the new saved-status pill.
