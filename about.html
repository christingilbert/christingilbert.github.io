About

Two disciplines of deciding what to leave out.

Availability Senior Experience Designer · Vancouver, BC · Open to senior individual-contributor roles in digital experience design, design systems and product design.
01 Surabaya, East Java, Indonesia

Fifteen tabs open on a laptop, in a hotel room in Surabaya, the sun going down in rhythm with the Maghrib prayer. Somewhere in one of those tabs was the fact that Ijen is the most acidic crater lake on earth, and that I was leaving for it at midnight.

I read the safety notes twice. Sulphur miners work that crater floor every day; I would be a visitor with a respirator, there to see a blue flame that only shows itself in the dark, then to watch the sun come up from a rim I hadn't reached yet.

That's usually how I decide what's next — more open tabs than any plan should need, and I go anyway.

02 East Java & Central Java

Childhood moved between East Java and Central Java — different houses, different neighbors, the same monsoon arriving on schedule every afternoon and the same call to prayer marking the hours before any clock did.

Information arrived through people and weather back then, not screens — who was visiting, what the sky was doing. I didn't call it research. It was just how you found out what was going to happen next.

Years later I went looking for the mechanism under all that noticing — a degree in clinical psychology, then a master's in cognitive neuroscience in Japan. How attention runs out. How memory drops what it can't hold. Why one screen wears you down and the next one doesn't. I don't open with it, but it sits under everything I design.

03 Digital Rez International · 2005

The first reservation interfaces I designed went live in 2005, for booking software used by hospitality clients — screens where someone committed real plans and real money, and where every extra field was a reason to abandon the cart. I was learning the same lesson at the same time behind a camera: what you frame is a claim about what matters, and everything outside the frame just disappears.

Fifteen-plus years on, I still think of them as the same job. Interfaces and photographs are both editing — deciding what to include, what to leave out. The leftover has to be enough, in both.

04 Himalayas · Kumano Kodo · Salish Sea

In 2026, as part of consulting work in Nepal, I interviewed more than ninety porters across the Himalayas for Voice of the Porters, a documentary scheduled for presentation to the Nepal Tourism Board. Most of what I learned came from waiting — for weather, for someone to decide I was worth the story.

The same instinct has sent me up the Kumano Kodo in Japan with twenty-six pounds on my shoulders, climbing out of Takijiri-oji into the outer bands of Typhoon Nakri, and out onto the Salish Sea the afternoon a mayday call cracked through Channel 16 and a quiet sail turned into scribbled coordinates.

None of it came with a UX checkbox attached. Go and look, stay long enough to be wrong about your first impression — that's the research instinct, and it predates the job title.

05 Éminence · 2020–2026

Éminence is where I learned that how people experience finished work depends on what's holding it up, not just what's on the screen. Six years there — first as a specialist, then running the digital creative team — I redesigned the DAM taxonomy and gave findable order to six thousand-plus assets. I also built a modular visual style guide and wrote the rules for what AI is and isn't allowed to generate.

None of that shows up in a finished campaign, which is the point. I ended up with my own definition of accessibility, not the textbook one: accessibility means who is able to use and work inside the system. Standards that hold under a deadline are the ones nobody has to think about — including the people building the next campaign, not only the people seeing it.

06 2005 → 2026

The first reservation interfaces I designed shipped in 2005. The prototype I'm building now, Nookfare, is a live prototype for choosing a stay — comparisons that lead with what's different, ratings that show their sources.

Two different decades, with a lot of unrelated work in between. And somehow the same moment on the screen: someone holding more than one option in mind, trying to commit to the right one.

I didn't plan the repeat. I noticed it while writing this page, which is probably the most accurate way to notice anything about your own work.

07 Vancouver, BC

These days that's Vancouver: sailing on the Salish Sea when the wind cooperates, mentoring designers and marketers in more than ten countries through ADPList, and working from a desk two cats consider theirs.

Open to senior individual-contributor roles in product design, digital experience design and creative technology, said plainly. Right now there's a laptop, a cold cup of tea, and a cat sitting on the keyboard shortcuts I actually need.
