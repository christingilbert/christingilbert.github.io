Himalayan Green Trips.

Helping travellers understand the trek ahead.

I designed and built a new website for Himalayan Green Trips to help travellers understand what to expect each day on the trail. The website connects daily itineraries with altitude profiles, while a custom CMS built with Sanity helps the agency keep trip information up to date.

01The starting point

Bringing the website closer to the agency’s work.

Himalayan Green Trips is a trekking agency in Kathmandu, led by Raj Shrestha. Alongside treks, the team supports porters and takes part in trail cleanups with Let’s Clean Up Nepal. I wanted visitors to learn about the agency’s approach while finding enough practical detail to choose a route.

The previous website left some important questions unanswered. Trek cards showed “Variable Rate(s)” and “Duration Varies,” offering little help when comparing routes. “Book Now” opened a message saying booking was unavailable, with no direct way to contact the team from the message.

My starting point was the journey from curiosity to an enquiry: what would someone want to know before asking about a trek? I also needed to consider how the agency would keep route information current after handover.

02Questions from the trail

“How much climbing is there tomorrow?”

Over dal bhat in Nepal, conversations often turned to distance and elevation. How far had we walked? How much climbing was left? I found myself asking my guide about the next day, trying to picture the effort ahead.

I’ve always been interested in the information people look for before feeling ready to make a decision. On a trek, a place name and a day number only tell part of the story. I wanted to bring walking time, elevation and the overnight stop together, so a traveller could understand a day without piecing together several parts of the page.

The conversations were informal, and my own experience shaped the early questions. I explored the design direction with the agency.

03The planning experience

What does a day on the trail look like?

Each itinerary day brings the walking details alongside an altitude profile. Distance and walking time sit with ascent, descent and accommodation. Choosing a day highlights the corresponding stops on the chart. Travellers can move forward or back through the route, choose a particular day, or read the full itinerary in order.

The profile also makes room for acclimatisation walks and daytime visits. The highest point reached during a day can be different from the overnight stop, an important distinction when picturing a trek.

Putting the journey on a calendar

I added a calendar so travellers could see the trek against a preferred arrival date. A preferred date starts the planning conversation; the agency still needs to confirm the booking.

Preparation continues away from the screen. A booking questionnaire helps travellers share plans with the team, while trek-specific packing tools support the days before departure. A downloadable PDF keeps the itinerary close at hand, and a QR code leads back to the website.

04The visual direction

Colour and landscape, drawn from the journey.

I drew the colour palette from the five colours of prayer flags, carrying a familiar part of the Himalayan landscape into the website. Mountain photography remains central, while light backgrounds give the trip information room to breathe.

The skyline became another way to bring the Khumbu landscape into the design. I built the illustration from four west–east slices of NASA SRTM 30 m elevation data, sampled through OpenTopodata. Published peak elevations provide the labelled heights, with source credits available beside the artwork.

The skyline gives a sense of the wider landscape. For day-to-day planning, the separate itinerary profile follows the elevations of the route’s stops.

05The editing system

A custom CMS built around the agency’s work.

A website needs to stay useful long after launch. Trek details change, and the person updating a route shouldn’t need to edit the same information in several places. I wanted to make everyday updates manageable for the agency.

I designed and built a custom content management system (CMS) with Sanity. The editor follows the journey day by day, with fields for distance and walking time, ascent and descent, accommodation and meals. A single update feeds the website’s itinerary, altitude profile and trip calendar, along with the downloadable PDF.

06The people behind the trek

Making room for the people behind the trek.

Guides and porters are part of the journey long before a traveller arrives in Nepal. I wanted the website to introduce the mountain team and give porter welfare a clear place in the planning experience.

Practical details matter here. The website covers porter load limits, base pay and insurance. The Everest Base Camp page states a maximum load of 20 kg per porter, and cleanup guidance sits alongside the trip information.

I kept the emphasis on the agency’s stated practices, giving travellers details to consider and questions to bring to the team. The work with Let’s Clean Up Nepal also has space alongside the trekking experience.

07Where the project stands

The website is live, with room to keep learning.

The website is now live, with planning tools that connect the route to the days ahead. The agency can maintain trip details through a custom CMS built with Sanity.

I led the strategy and design, from the content structure to the visual direction. I also built the front end and Sanity integration with support from Claude Code and Codex. I remained responsible for the design decisions and final review.

I’d like to understand more about how travellers use the planning tools and where questions remain before an enquiry. The live website gives the agency a starting point for learning from real visits.
